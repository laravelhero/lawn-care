Spring is the most important season for your lawn. What you do (and don't do) in March and April sets the trajectory for the entire year. Here in the Triangle, our transitional climate means timing matters more than almost anywhere — move too early and you waste product, move too late and weeds win the race.

Here's the exact checklist our crews follow to wake a lawn up the right way.

## 1. Start with a thorough cleanup

Before anything else, clear the debris that winter left behind. Rake out matted leaves, fallen twigs, and dead thatch. Matted material traps moisture against the crown of the grass, invites fungal disease, and blocks the sunlight your turf is desperate for after dormancy.

A light spring cleanup also lets you see what you're working with — bare spots, compacted areas, and early weed pressure all become obvious once the surface is clear.

## 2. Test your soil before you feed

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the one that matters most. Piedmont clay soils tend to run acidic, and acidic soil locks up the nutrients in fertilizer so your grass can't absorb them.

A simple soil test tells you:

- Your soil pH (and whether you need lime)
- Existing nutrient levels
- What your lawn actually needs — instead of guessing

> Testing before treating is the single biggest difference between a lawn that limps along and one that thrives. Never feed blind.

## 3. Apply pre-emergent at the right time

Pre-emergent herbicide stops crabgrass and other annual weeds *before* they germinate. But it only works if it's down before soil temperatures hit roughly 55°F — which in the Triangle usually means early-to-mid March.

Miss the window and the barrier is useless for the season. This is the most timing-sensitive task on the entire checklist, so don't put it off.

## 4. Sharpen your mower blade

A dull blade tears grass instead of slicing it, leaving ragged tips that brown out and invite disease. Sharpen (or replace) your blade at the start of every season, and aim to sharpen again mid-summer if you mow a large lawn.

When you make that first cut, follow the **one-third rule**: never remove more than a third of the blade height in a single mow.

## 5. Feed for root growth, not just color

Your first feeding should support root development, not just push a quick flush of green. A balanced, slow-release fertilizer feeds the lawn steadily over weeks rather than dumping nitrogen all at once.

Strong spring roots are what carry your lawn through the brutal Triangle summer ahead.

## 6. Address bare and thin spots

Spring is a great time to overseed thin areas (especially for fescue lawns) and to plan core aeration if your soil is compacted. Aeration relieves compaction, improves water and oxygen movement, and gives new seed the soil contact it needs to establish.

## Your quick spring checklist

- [x] Clear leaves, debris, and matted thatch
- [x] Pull a soil test and correct pH if needed
- [x] Apply pre-emergent before soil hits 55°F
- [x] Sharpen the mower blade
- [x] Apply a slow-release, root-focused feeding
- [x] Overseed thin spots and aerate compacted areas

Get these six things right and your lawn will reward you with thick, deep-green turf all season long.
